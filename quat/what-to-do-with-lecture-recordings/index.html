What to do with your lecture recordings

Having the lecture recorded buys a reassurance that usually costs you. The feeling that you already have it postpones the work indefinitely, and by the end of term there are forty hours of audio nobody is going to play — because re-listening to a lecture costs exactly as much time as attending it did and teaches you rather less.

That is the real problem with recorded lectures. It is not that they are useless. It is that in their original form they can only be consumed in real time.

The rule everything else follows from

A recording is not study material. It is raw material. It only starts being worth something once it has been turned into something you can move through faster than real time: an outline, a summary, a handful of questions.

So the useful question is not "when will I listen to this", which never arrives, but "what am I taking out of it and how long will that take".

What to do, in order

Do not replay it end to end. Unless you missed the lecture, this is the worst time-to-benefit ratio available. If you did miss it, play it once at 1.5x or 2x while taking notes, and treat that as having attended.

Go straight to the places you marked. If you take notes during class, write the timestamp whenever something does not land. Five timestamps turn a one-hour recording into six minutes of work. It is the highest-yield habit on this page and it costs two seconds a mark.

Get a transcript if you can. Text can be searched, skimmed and scanned. Most university capture systems — Panopto, Echo360, Teams — already generate captions or a transcript, so look for one before assuming there is none. A transcript turns an hour of audio into something you review in ten minutes.

Extract questions, not summaries. A summary gets read once and forgotten. What survives is a handful of questions you can get wrong. An hour of lecture yields somewhere between fifteen and forty reasonable cards; if you are producing a hundred, you are transcribing rather than selecting.

Record which lecture each thing came from. In two months you will look at a card and be unable to place it, and without the source you cannot check it against the recording you still have sitting there.

Then delete the audio once you have wrung it out. If you already have the questions, the file is dead weight that makes you feel in debt.

When it is not worth it

If the lecture came with slides or a script, working from the document is almost always the better trade: it reads faster, it is searchable, and it usually contains the same content. Keep the recording for the ten minutes where the lecturer explained something that is on no slide — which tends to be exactly what the exam asks about.
